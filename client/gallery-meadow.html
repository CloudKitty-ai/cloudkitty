Every slider writes VIEW.meadow, which is the same object the live renderer reads — so this draws the shipped code, not a copy of it. Cats are here for scale only. Paste the readout into both anim.js and meadow.js to bake.

Occlusion — a cat walking past a shrub

The same cat at five depths relative to one shrub, drawn through the renderer's own sort keys (catSortKey / coverSortKey in meadow.js, shared so this and the live page cannot disagree). The cat should be hidden at −1 and in front at +1; where it flips is bushBase, and how much there is to hide behind is bushLift. Set bushLift to 0 and nothing occludes at all — the shrub no longer leaves its tile.

Ponds — depth, lip, caustics, meniscus

The three shapes this world actually has, drawn through the shipped buildPondLayers and drawPonds. The blur is clamped by each pond’s own inradius: a blur wider than the pond has nothing to be deep in the middle of, and at a flat 0.95 a lone tile reaches 18% depth and even the 2×2 lake only 49%. The number under each shape is what it actually got. The river is here because area-based sizing would call it as roomy as the lake, and it is the blob that most needs a tight blur.

The same render at 3× — a magnifier, not a bigger draw. Every pixel below is a pixel from the strip above, blown up with smoothing off, so the aliasing on a 1.7px meniscus is the aliasing that ships. Drawing at a 3× tile would flatter the art: it would show a 93px tile, and the world’s largest is 54.

All four phases at once, each with a lone tile and the 2×2 lake, since the phase buttons only ever show you one. Night is the one to look at: measured, its water separates from the ground less than half as well as day’s (contrast 1.29 against 1.98), while the shore-to-deep ramp runs more than twice as strong and the meniscus goes from invisible against the ground to a 5.3 rim. The pale ring and dark centre are what “reads as a hole” looks like.

Meniscus — where a cat meets the water, right through the day

The MENISCUS dials, drawn through the shipped drawWaterline and the shipped clip — the same waterlineFor / surfaceForPose the renderer uses, so what is cut here is what is cut live. Four phases because the line takes its colour from pondMeniscus, which is a per-theme entry: this is the card that catches a value tuned at noon and unreadable at midnight. Night is the one to judge — a fixed mix toward white measured 33 points of L* too bright there, which is why the palette is asked rather than told.

Drawn at the camera tile a WQHD display produces — about 100px, from a ~1000px map divided by the 10 tiles the camera frames. Deliberately not the Scale dial: that dial describes the WHOLE-WORLD tile, and its default of 31 is a laptop's. Doubling it gave 62px, which on a WQHD display is barely above the 48–53px that display already shows without a camera at all — so the card was judging the meniscus at almost exactly the size it was parked for being too small to judge at.

Every MENISCUS value is a FRACTION of the tile, so what changes between displays is only how many pixels the line gets: the stroke is tile × 0.045, which is 4.5px here and about 2px on a laptop's ~45px camera tile. Dial it here, then sanity-check it against the 3× magnifier strip above, which is where sub-pixel aliasing shows.

Each cell is the same cat standing in the middle of the same 2×2 lake, so the only thing varying across the strip is the palette. Down it, only the submersion changes: 0.45 on the top row, 0.95 on the bottom.

That difference is almost entirely alpha, not height, which is worth knowing before dialling. The waterline sits 81px down her 100px box at 0.45 and 73px at 0.95 — barely 8px apart, because waterline (0.72) and her ground line (0.88) are only 0.16 of a box apart. What submersion really drives is strength: the line goes 0.19 → 0.40 and the fill 0.07 → 0.15, a little over double each. So a value that reads well on the bottom row can be invisible on the top one, and the top row is the harder test.

Leaf swing — the same shrub, right through the day

The lobed shrub's four leaf ticks mark the lit side, so they slide with the sun: their centre is −shadowLean × r × bushLeafSwing, and because the four are evenly spaced their own offsets cancel exactly, leaving a clean sideways displacement. Only dawn and dusk lean far (+0.8 and −0.85); day and night sit near zero, so the dial looks dead there and this strip is the only place to judge it. The owner saw these as two separate faults a day apart — “off centre left” one evening, “off centre right” the next — which is one dial seen from both ends. The lobes and the trunk do NOT move with the ticks, so past some swing the motif stops reading as light and starts reading as a part that came loose. The hairline is the crown's centre and the dot is the ticks' measured centre — measured off the actual draw, not recomputed, so this cannot drift from what ships. At 0 the dot sits on the line in every phase and the gradient carries the light alone.

Ground cover — the four styles, same seed

All four bake into the ground cache, which sits under everything: nothing in the renderer y-sorts, so a cat walking across one draws on top of it. Anything that reads as standing up off the ground reads wrong the moment that happens — which is the whole reason cover lies flat. Each panel below draws a cat over a clump so you can judge that directly.

Drifts — the same cover, gathered into places

The left panel is the OLD placement: every tile rolled its own dice, so density looked the same through any window you put over it. The right one is the same world with the fertility field on. The counts are identical — the readout under them says so, and if it ever stops saying so that is a bug, not a taste call. What changes is only where cover lands.
